# Case studies: can a district measurably grow or recover enrollment against charters and private schools?

Prepared 2026-10-08 for a Minneapolis Public Schools (MPS) enrollment plan. MPS context: about 29,000 students; 42% of resident students attend charters or other districts; enrollment down from about 36,000 (2015); target is about 3,000 net new students over 3 years.

Sources were gathered with WebSearch and WebFetch. Vendor claims are flagged. "Evidence" means how strongly the data supports the claim that district action caused the enrollment change. "Similarity" means how close the district is to MPS: urban, high charter share, declining enrollment.

## Bottom line

- **No well-documented US case shows an urban district with heavy charter competition adding about 10% net enrollment in 3 years through recruitment or marketing alone.** The best documented recoveries come from three sources:
  1. Citywide population growth plus universal pre-K (DC).
  2. Systemwide quality, governance and levy reform that stabilized enrollment and held market share (Cleveland).
  3. Immigration inflows (Newark, and part of St. Paul in 2024-25).
- **Recruitment campaigns are cheap relative to per-pupil revenue.** They show real gains at targeted schools, often 7-50%. District-wide effects are small, measured in hundreds of students, and hard to separate from regional trends.
- **Ranking, weighing strength of evidence against similarity to MPS:**
  1. **DC Public Schools (DCPS):** strongest evidence, moderate similarity. It is the best proof that a pre-K pipeline and enrollment growth can coexist with about 47% charter share.
  2. **Saint Paul Public Schools (SPPS):** highest similarity, moderate-to-low causal evidence. It is the best local playbook and cost benchmark, and also a warning: its gains were partly regional and partly reversed.
  3. **Cleveland Metropolitan School District (CMSD):** moderate evidence, high similarity. A systemwide plan stopped a long decline and held market share against charters for about 6 years.
- Detroit is a useful supporting case. Newark, Miami-Dade and Des Moines are cautionary or benchmark cases.

## 1. DC Public Schools (Washington, DC): growth alongside a large charter sector, anchored by universal pre-K

**What they did**
- **Pre-K:** Universal, free pre-K for 3- and 4-year-olds under the Pre-K Enhancement and Expansion Amendment Act of 2008. By 2022-23, 79% of DC 3-year-olds were in public pre-K, at about $22,207 per child (FutureEd).
- **Choice:** A common lottery (My School DC) covering DCPS and charters.
- **Investment:** Large facility modernization spending and mayoral-control reforms after 2007. The modernization dollar figure was not verified in this pass.
- **Closures:** In 2008 Chancellor Rhee closed about 23 schools. That count is from background knowledge and was not re-fetched.

**Measured result** (OSSE-audited series published by the Mayor's office)

| School year | DCPS | Charter | Charter share of public enrollment |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2007-08 | 49,422 | 21,947 | ~31% |
| 2008-09 | 45,190 | 25,729 | ~36% |
| 2009-10 (low point) | 44,718 | 27,661 | |
| 2016-17 | 48,555 | 41,506 | 46% |
| 2019-20 | 51,037 | 43,518 | |
| 2024-25 (preliminary) | 52,036 | 47,564 | ~48% |

- DCPS grew from its 2009-10 low of 44,718 to 52,036 in 2024-25: **+7,318 students (+16%)**.
- **Counterfactual caveat:** total public enrollment grew about 41% over the same period (70,919 to 99,770), and the city's under-5 population rose by about 10,000 from 2010 to 2015. DCPS's share of public enrollment fell from about 64% to about 52%. So DCPS grew in absolute terms by riding citywide growth and pre-K. It did not win share back from charters.
- **Recent trend:** total DC enrollment slipped 0.1% in 2025-26, driven by a 3% drop in pre-K (DC Policy Center).
- **Strongest causal evidence: the pre-K-to-K pipeline.** Braga et al. (2024) used DC's randomized lottery. Children who won a Pre-K3 seat were:
  - up to 16.8 percentage points more likely to enroll in a DC public kindergarten;
  - up to 35 percentage points more likely to progress through Pre-K4 into kindergarten;
  - 17.8 percentage points less likely to switch schools between Pre-K4 and kindergarten;
  - more likely to stay in the public system through grade 2.
- Effects were largest in low-income neighborhoods, in communities of color and in dual-language programs.
- **Closures signal:** in the year after the 2008 closures, DCPS fell by 4,232 students (-8.6%) while charters gained 3,782. This is correlational, but the timing is suggestive.

**Cost:** About $22,207 per pre-K child per year (2022-23). This is a full program cost, not a marketing cost.

**Confidence:** HIGH that DCPS enrollment grew and that pre-K causally raises retention into district kindergarten. MED-LOW that DCPS beat charters, because it lost share.

**Similarity to MPS:** MED.
- Similar: urban, about 48% charter share, a past enrollment collapse.
- Different: a growing child population, much higher per-pupil spending, mayoral control, and a citywide lottery.

**Sources**
- Mayor's office enrollment table (2024): https://mayor.dc.gov/release/mayor-bowser-highlights-continued-growth-public-school-enrollment-ahead-2024-edfest
- Mayor's office enrollment table (2023): https://dc.gov/release/mayor-bowser-highlights-continued-growth-public-school-enrollment
- Deputy Mayor for Education (DME) enrollment trends 2011-2016: https://dme.dc.gov/sites/default/files/dc/sites/dme/publication/attachments/Public%20School%20Enrollment%20Trends%202011-2016%20FINAL.pdf
- Braga et al., "The Effects of Public Pre-K for 3-year-olds…", EdWorkingPaper 24-1019: https://edworkingpapers.com/ai24-1019
- FutureEd summary of Braga et al.: https://www.future-ed.org/research-notes-the-impact-of-pre-k3-programs-on-elementary-enrollments-student-supports/
- DC Policy Center, 2025-26 enrollment: https://www.dcpolicycenter.org/publications/chart-of-the-week-enrollment-trends-for-2026/

**Lessons for Minneapolis.** DC's best transferable lesson is the pipeline, not the marketing. A family that wins a district Pre-K3 seat is far more likely to stay for kindergarten and beyond, especially in dual-language programs and low-income neighborhoods, and those are exactly the MPS segments with the highest charter leakage. MPS reported 941 preschool students in 2022-23, and many no-cost seats went unfilled. Filling and expanding Pre-K3/Pre-K4 seats in schools that have kindergarten capacity is the most evidence-backed lever available.

DC also shows the limits. Even with universal pre-K and heavy capital investment, DCPS lost market share to charters. MPS should plan on pre-K slowing exits, not on reversing them alone. The 2008 DCPS closures were followed by a one-year loss of about 4,200 students, so any MPS consolidation should be paired with guaranteed seats in strong receiving schools.

## 2. Saint Paul Public Schools (MN): targeted "Enrollment Project", identity-based programs, pre-K

**What they did**
- **2018:** Mayor- and teachers-union-led door-knocking. Goals were 10,000 doors and 2,500 conversations. Cost was $41,590 ($31,590 from AFT, $10,000 from SPPS) (FOX 9).
- **2022:** $300,000 two-year marketing campaign from the general fund, mostly passed to low-enrollment schools (American Experiment, Nov 2022).
- **Consolidation:** "Envision SPPS" closed and merged schools, but kept three immigrant-serving schools open.
- **New and renamed identity programs:**
  - Txuj Ci HMong Language and Culture Upper Campus (2022)
  - East African Elementary Magnet (2023)
  - Riverview renamed to highlight Spanish/English dual immersion
  - Expanded Spanish immersion and Karen classes
- **Pre-K and transport:** Pre-K expansion of 1,470 free full-day seats in 2022-23, with 800+ families waitlisted. Yellow buses restored for high schoolers.
- **The Enrollment Project (Aug 2023 onward, with vendor SchoolMint):** 6 under-enrolled elementary and community schools received money and staff support. Tactics included:
  - "secret shopper" audits;
  - customer-service and tour training for principals;
  - multilingual Facebook and Instagram ads;
  - school microsites;
  - follow-up with interested families.
- **Spending on the Enrollment Project:** $350,000 in the first two years and $75,000 in year three (Star Tribune, Mar 2025).

**Measured result**

| Period | District-wide change | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Each of the 4 years to fall 2022 | Lost more than 1,000 students per year | Sahan Journal |
| Fall 2022 (preliminary count) | -3.7% | American Experiment |
| Fall 2022 to fall 2023 | -48 students (-0.1%) | Sahan Journal, Jan 2024 |
| Oct 1, 2024 | **31,496 K-12, +2% (about +600)**, the first increase in about a decade | MDE via Star Tribune |
| Oct 2024 to Mar 2026 | **About -900 net, including about 500 English learners**, after the federal immigration enforcement surge in the Twin Cities ("Operation Metro Surge") | Sahan Journal, Aug 2026 |

- SchoolMint (vendor) projected +527 students for 2024-25.
- **Target-school gains, fall 2024, preliminary district figures:**
  - Txuj Ci Upper +48%
  - East African Magnet +58% (outside the Enrollment Project)
  - Riverview +16%, plus one extra kindergarten section
  - Highwood Hills +16%
  - Cherokee Heights +9%
  - Dayton's Bluff +7%
  - Hamline -7%
- Two years earlier, the Txuj Ci lower campus was up more than a third and Adams Spanish Immersion was up more than a quarter.
- **Counterfactual caveats:**
  1. Minneapolis also posted a "slight" gain in fall 2024, driven by Latino immigration. The Star Tribune names a rise in Hispanic students as the main driver in both cities, which points to a regional factor rather than an SPPS-specific campaign effect.
  2. Part of Txuj Ci's growth is structural: it absorbed another Hmong program and added grades.
  3. The 2024-25 gain partly reversed in 2025-26 when immigrant families left.

**Cost and cost per student**
- About $425,000 over 3 years for the Enrollment Project, before staff time and federal relief funds used for pre-K.
- If the project produced even 100-200 incremental students, the cost is about $2,000-$4,000 per student. That is well below a year of per-pupil revenue, assumed here at about $15,000 or more in Minnesota (not verified this pass). This is an inference: no causal estimate exists.

**Confidence:**
- HIGH that the decline stopped in fall 2023-24 and turned up in 2024-25 (MDE data).
- MED that targeted schools gained because of the campaign.
- LOW that the campaign caused district-wide growth, because Minneapolis rose too, the gain was immigration-sensitive, and it was not durable.

**Similarity to MPS:** VERY HIGH. Same metro, same state funding and open-enrollment rules, similar charter leakage (60% of SPPS leavers went to charters), similar size.

**Sources**
- KSTP, Nov 2024: https://kstp.com/kstp-news/top-news/spps-sees-increase-in-enrollment-for-first-time-in-a-decade/
- Star Tribune, Mar 2025: https://www.startribune.com/minneapolis-st-paul-school-enrollment-increases/601236788
- Sahan Journal, Jan 2024: https://sahanjournal.com/education/st-paul-schools-halt-enrollment-slide-hmong-spanish-east-african-programs/
- Sahan Journal, Aug 2026: https://sahanjournal.com/education/minnesota-schools-lost-1700-students-after-metro-surge/
- **VENDOR CLAIM**, SchoolMint case study: https://schoolmint.com/reversing-a-decade-of-declining-student-enrollment-at-saint-paul-public-schools/
- FOX 9 (2018 door-knocking): https://www.fox9.com/news/st-paul-schools-look-to-bolster-enrollment-with-door-knocking-campaign
- American Experiment (2022 spend): https://www.americanexperiment.org/st-paul-school-district-spending-big-to-recruit-students/
- City of Minneapolis memo comparing SPPS and MPS: https://www.minneapolismn.gov/media/-www-content-assets/documents/Public-School-Enrollment-Incentives.pdf

**Lessons for Minneapolis.** SPPS is the closest playbook, and its evidence points in a consistent direction:
- **What clearly worked was at the school level.** Identity-based programs (Hmong, East African, Spanish immersion) grew 16-58% at the schools that offered them. Front-door basics (tours, follow-up, multilingual ads, secret-shopper fixes) are cheap, at about $140,000 per year for 6 schools.
- **District-wide, the realistic yield is hundreds, not thousands.** The best SPPS year was about +600, and it was helped by immigration that also lifted Minneapolis.
- **Gains from immigrant families are fragile.** They reversed when enforcement drove families away.
- **Implications for MPS:**
  - Copy the targeted-school model and the identity and dual-language programs.
  - Measure each school against a matched non-target school, which SPPS never did.
  - Do not book more than about 1-2% a year district-wide from recruitment.
  - Include retention of English-learner families in the plan, not only recruitment.

## 3. Cleveland Metropolitan School District (OH): the Cleveland Plan stopped a 10-year collapse and held share against charters

**What they did**
- **2012 Cleveland Plan** (state law HB 525, June 2012), followed by Issue 107, a 15-mill operating levy passed in November 2012. It was the district's first new operating levy in 16 years.
- **Portfolio model:** about 20 autonomous district and charter schools in 2013, with a goal to triple portfolio enrollment. New magnet and choice high schools.
- **Charter partnership:** CMSD shares local levy revenue with high-performing "partnering" charters.
- **Pre-K:** 750 new high-quality preschool seats. High-quality preschool enrollment rose from 2,857 to 4,903 between 2013 and 2019.
- **Other elements:** a parent information campaign (the "Cleveland Transformation Alliance" school-quality guide) and a later attendance marketing campaign (2017-18).
- An earlier door-to-door recruitment effort was described as unsuccessful (WPR, 2018).

**Measured result**
- CMSD lost almost 50% of its enrollment from 2002 to 2011, falling to under 35,000 (WPR).
- After the plan, the Transformation Alliance's 2019 report shows:

| School year | CMSD | All Cleveland charters | CMSD share of CMSD + charters |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2012-13 | 38,724 | 14,637 | 72.6% |
| 2014-15 | 38,555 | 16,596 (peak) | |
| 2015-16 | 39,125 (peak) | 15,989 | |
| 2018-19 | 37,701 | 14,676 | 72.0% |

- WPR reports enrollment rose "about 5%" after the plan.
- **Definitions caveat:** the WPR figures ("under 35,000" in 2011) and the Alliance figures (38,724 in 2012-13) likely use different definitions, for example whether preschool is counted. Pull Ohio Department of Education data before citing.
- **Counterfactual:** Cleveland's population kept falling over this period, and charters lost about 2,000 students from their 2014-15 peak. Holding flat with stable market share in a shrinking city is a real but modest achievement. There is no formal counterfactual study.

**Cost:** A 15-mill levy. The annual dollar yield was not verified in this pass; check CMSD levy documents. The plan was also philanthropy-backed, with more than $4 million from the Cleveland Foundation for implementation.

**Confidence:** MED. Official enrollment series show stabilization and share retention, but attribution to the plan is plausible rather than proven.

**Similarity to MPS:** HIGH. Rust Belt city, about 28% charter share, a long decline, a levy-dependent budget, and a district willing to partner with charters.

**Sources**
- Cleveland Transformation Alliance 2019 progress report: https://mycleschool.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/Alliance-Community-Progress-Report_2019-11.4-PAGES.pdf
- Cleveland Foundation, Cleveland Plan overview: https://www.clevelandfoundation100.org/impact/the-cleveland-plan-for-transforming-schools/
- WPR, 2018 (Cleveland comparison): https://www.wpr.org/education/declining-enrollment-milwaukee-public-schools-leaders-get-creative-boost-student-numbers
- Fordham Institute review of the Cleveland Plan: https://fordhaminstitute.org/ohio/commentary/progress-and-problems-checking-cleveland-plan

**Lessons for Minneapolis.** Cleveland stopped a steep decline not with ads but with a credible, citywide quality story:
- a cross-sector plan;
- a new levy;
- new choice and magnet high schools;
- a published school-quality guide;
- a pre-K expansion.

Its door-to-door recruitment reportedly did little. The realistic outcome was stabilization and holding about 72% share for 6 years, not a 10% rebound. For MPS this argues for a single public plan covering quality, programs and pre-K, with the enrollment campaign layered on top. A purely marketing-led effort lacks this precedent.

## 4. Detroit Public Schools Community District (MI): sustained recruitment and retention machine, modest gains (supporting case)

**What they did** (under Superintendent Vitti, 2017 onward)
- Pre-K expansion: 17 new classrooms at 12 schools in 2023.
- Canvassing: about 78,000 homes in summer 2025, with about 40 contracted canvassers.
- "Summer on the Block" events (about 5,000 attendees) and back-to-school expos.
- Billboards, TV and radio.
- Kindergarten boot camp.
- A school-level re-enrollment-rate metric used for principal accountability.
- Funded canvassing to bring no-show students into class.
- About $3.5 million marketing budget in 2025.

**Measured result**
- K-12 enrollment went from 45.5K (2016) to about 51K (2019), per district claims. The 2017 jump was largely the return of students from the dissolved state turnaround district, the Education Achievement Authority, so it was not organic.
- Fall-start counts: about 52,300 (2022), about 51,600 (2023), about 53,044 (2024). These are early-year figures, not the state's official Count Day.
- **Count Day** (the official state funding count, held in October): 44,893 (2024) to 45,388 (2025), **+495 (+1.1%)**.
- The district regained about 1,000 of about 3,000 students lost during COVID.
- **Canvassing funnel:** 532 leads produced about 80 enrollments by mid-August 2025, a lead-to-enrollment yield of about 15%.
- Vitti says the main problem is retention, not recruitment.

**Cost:** About $3.5 million per year in marketing. Against +495 on Count Day, that is about $7,000 per net student. That figure overstates the cost if gross new enrollments are counted instead of the net.

**Confidence:** MED for a modest net gain since 2024 and stabilization since 2017. LOW for attribution.

**Similarity to MPS:** HIGH. About 50% charter share, a long decline, a poor city.

**Sources**
- Chalkbeat, Count Day 2025: https://www.chalkbeat.org/detroit/2025/10/03/officials-say-detroit-school-districts-2025-count-day-numbers-up-by-500
- Chalkbeat via BridgeDetroit, Aug 2025: https://www.bridgedetroit.com/the-detroit-school-district-is-ramping-up-enrollment-and-student-retention-efforts-will-it-pay-off/
- Chalkbeat, Sep 2023: https://www.chalkbeat.org/detroit/2023/9/21/23883994/detroit-public-schools-charters-declining-enrollment
- Chalkbeat, Sep 2024: https://www.chalkbeat.org/detroit/2024/09/13/detroit-public-schools-enrollment-shows-promise
- Chalkbeat, Oct 2019: https://www.chalkbeat.org/posts/detroit/2019/10/21/vitti-says-enrollment-is-up-in-the-detroit-school-district/

**Lessons for Minneapolis.** Detroit is what a large, sustained campaign buys:
- **Cost and scale:** about $3.5 million a year, tens of thousands of doors, and event programming for a net gain of about 500 students a year at best.
- **Benchmarks:** a lead-to-enrollment yield of about 15% and a cost of several thousand dollars per net student. Both are useful for sizing an MPS budget.
- **The most transferable element is the re-enrollment metric.** Holding each principal accountable for the share of families who return each spring makes retention a measured school-level job.

## 5. Cautionary and benchmark cases

**Newark Public Schools (NJ)**
- Enrollment rose from about 36,600 (2019) to almost 44,000 (2024-25), about +20%, versus +0.39% statewide. District enrollment passed charter enrollment in 2023.
- Growth was almost entirely among Hispanic students and English learners (about 6x more English learners than in 2019). That points to immigration, not recruitment.
- The district hired Caissa Public Strategy on a **pay-per-enrollment contract: $890 per student enrolled, capped at $276,790 for up to 311 students, 2024-26**. Earlier targeted schools gained 75-82 students each from 2022 to 2024.
- The district lost more than 700 students in 2025-26 amid immigration enforcement.
- Confidence: HIGH on the numbers, LOW on campaign attribution. The $890-per-enrollment price is a useful market benchmark; Caissa's "$6,500 per student" ROI framing is a **vendor claim**.
- Sources:
  - https://www.chalkbeat.org/newark/2024/06/24/newark-public-schools-rehires-caissa-public-strategy-for-student-recruitment-services
  - https://www.chalkbeat.org/newark/2025/10/02/newark-school-enrollment-grows-changing-student-demographics-language-needs/
  - https://www.chalkbeat.org/newark/2026/06/03/charter-traditional-public-schools-seeing-fewer-students-where-are-they-going/

**Miami-Dade County Public Schools (FL)**
- The most aggressive district choice expansion in the US:
  - students in district-run choice programs rose from 35% (2010) to 61% (2018-19);
  - magnet enrollment rose from 41,251 to 72,194 across 114 magnets;
  - under-enrolled buildings were converted to magnets instead of closed.
- Yet district-run enrollment still fell from 321,279 to 281,969 over the decade, while charters rose from 7% to 20% share. Total enrollment then fell about 12,000 more in 2025-26 as Florida vouchers expanded.
- Choice programs kept families who would otherwise have left, but there is no evidence of net recovery.
- Confidence: HIGH on the numbers, and the record does not show net growth.
- Sources:
  - Education Next (author Ron Matus works for Step Up For Students, a voucher organization): https://www.educationnext.org/miami-choice-tsunami-carvalho-competition-transformation-miami-dade/
  - Axios, Aug 2025: https://www.axios.com/local/miami/2025/08/25/miami-public-schools-lose-12000-students-enrollment-down

**Des Moines Public Schools (IA)**
- About $300,000 per year in marketing ("Come here. Become here."), grant funded, with the second contract targeting the elementary-to-middle transition.
- The annual decline slowed from about 900 to about 400 students, but the district says it cannot attribute that to marketing.
- Source: https://www.edweek.org/leadership/public-schools-launch-marketing-campaigns-to-compete-with-school-choice/2024/08

**Duval County Public Schools (FL)**
- $110,000-120,000 TV campaign in 2022 aimed at charter families. No results were reported.
- Source: https://actionnewsjax.com/news/local/dcps-advertising-attract-families-district-competes-with-private-charter-schools/DO2EJH7UFZE4FPLEDXGOITCBUM

**Milwaukee Public Schools (WI)**
- 2018 door-knocking campaign. MPS had grown about 2,000 students (about 3%) over the prior two years. The campaign's own effect was never reported.
- Source: https://www.wpr.org/education/declining-enrollment-milwaukee-public-schools-leaders-get-creative-boost-student-numbers

## Cross-cutting research

**Effectiveness and cost of district marketing**
- **No rigorous causal evidence was found that district marketing raises net enrollment.** A 2023 systematic review (Greaves, Wilson & Nairn, *Review of Educational Research*) found the literature mostly qualitative. It concludes that whether marketing changes choice is an open question, and notes that marketing is often a substitute for program change. https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.3102/00346543221141658
- **Cost per enrolled student benchmarks:**

| Case | Spend | Result | Cost per student |
|---|---|---|---|
| Newark pay-per-enrollment contract | $890 per student enrolled | Gross enrollments, not proven incremental | $890 (contract price) |
| SPPS Enrollment Project | About $140,000 per year for 6 schools | Unknown incremental gain | About $2,000-4,000 if 100-200 students are incremental (inference) |
| Detroit | About $3.5 million per year | +495 net on Count Day | About $7,000 per net student |
| Detroit canvassing funnel | n/a | 532 leads, about 80 enrolled | About 15% lead-to-enrollment yield |

- Each retained or recruited student brings a full year of per-pupil revenue, often for many years. At those prices, even modest yields pay back, provided the measurement is honest.

**Pre-K to K retention within the same district**
- **Strongest evidence:** the DC lottery study. Winning a Pre-K3 seat raised the probability of enrolling in a DC public kindergarten by up to 16.8 percentage points, and cut school switching between Pre-K4 and K by 17.8 percentage points (Braga et al., 2024). https://edworkingpapers.com/ai24-1019
- **Secondary:** in Miami-Dade, 78% of children in public-school pre-K stayed at the same school for kindergarten, as cited in the City of Minneapolis memo (Miami School Readiness Project). Chicago full-day pre-K reportedly raised enrollment and attendance.
- **Local data point:** MPS had 941 pre-K students in 2022-23, with unfilled no-cost seats. SPPS had 1,470 free seats with 800 or more families waitlisted.

**Grade 8 to 9 retention**
- No rigorous program evaluation of a district campaign to keep 8th graders for district high school was found. This is an evidence gap.
- **Adjacent evidence:**
  - Des Moines retargeted its campaign at the elementary-to-middle transition after diagnosing losses there.
  - Detroit uses a school-level re-enrollment rate.
  - Cleveland added magnet and choice high schools.
- **Recommendation:** MPS should treat grades 5-to-6 and 8-to-9 as measured transition points, tracking the share of 8th graders who enroll in an MPS 9th grade by feeder school. Pilot with a matched comparison.

**Closures and district-wide family trust**
- **Finances:** Pearman (Stanford, Sep 2026) studied all California districts, 2011-2019, using synthetic difference-in-differences. Districts that closed schools did not improve their finances, because closures cut revenue mainly through enrollment loss, an average of about 288 students over the 10-year window. In large urban districts the revenue drop was about $845 per pupil. https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2026/09/california-school-closures-financial-impact-research
- **Counter-evidence:** Kim (2024) studied 470 Texas closures and found no evidence that students left the Texas public system after a closure (differential attrition of 0.5 percentage points or less). Texas has a lower charter share and the study measures the state system, not the district. https://edworkingpapers.com/sites/default/files/ai24-963.pdf
- **Trust:** a Research for Action synthesis (2024) finds trust effects are mostly qualitative. Families report losing trusted relationships, communities perceive decisions as predetermined, and careful planning is the main mitigator. https://www.researchforaction.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/revisiting-research-on-school-closings-key-learnings-for-district-and-community-leaders.pdf
- **Receiving schools:** they see small, persistent negative effects. Displaced students do better only when they land in higher-performing schools. https://fordhaminstitute.org/national/commentary/impact-school-closures-deep-dive-research
- **Case signal:** DCPS fell 8.6% the year after the 2008 closures while charters grew. SPPS deliberately kept immigrant-serving schools open during consolidation, and those schools then led its growth.

## Ranked summary (evidence x similarity)

| Rank | Case | Evidence | Similarity | Net enrollment result | Cost signal |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | DC Public Schools | HIGH (audited data + lottery-based causal pre-K study) | MED | +7,318 (+16%) from 2009-10 to 2024-25; lost share to charters | About $22K per pre-K seat |
| 2 | Saint Paul Public Schools | MED school-level, LOW district-level | VERY HIGH | +2% (about +600) in 2024-25, then about -900 by Mar 2026 | About $425K over 3 years |
| 3 | Cleveland Metropolitan | MED | HIGH | Flat at about 38-39K, 72% share held, 2012-2018 | 15-mill levy + philanthropy |
| 4 | Detroit Public Schools Community District | MED-LOW | HIGH | +495 Count Day (2025) | About $3.5M per year marketing |
| — | Newark / Miami-Dade / Des Moines | Benchmarks or cautions | MED | Immigration-driven / no net recovery / slower decline | $890 per enrollment / n/a / $300K per year |

**Implication for the 3,000-student target:**
- Recruitment-only precedents support about 1-2% a year district-wide, roughly 300-600 students a year for MPS.
- Reaching about 3,000 in 3 years likely requires stacking several levers:
  - pre-K pipeline expansion and kindergarten capture;
  - identity and dual-language programs;
  - systematic re-enrollment and transition-grade retention;
  - avoiding closure-driven exits.
- Even then, no comparable urban district in this review achieved a net +10% in 3 years without population or immigration tailwinds.
