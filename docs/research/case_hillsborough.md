# Case study: Hillsborough County Public Schools (Tampa, FL), plus Tampa Bay and Florida recruiter contracts

Researched 2026-10-08 with web search and fetch only. Tampa Bay Times (TBT) pages are paywalled, so for several TBT stories only the headline could be read. Their figures here come from WUSF, Fox13, Tampa Bay Beacons and syndicated copies.

## Verdict: NOT SUPPORTED, with one small exception

The evidence does not show that HCPS increased enrollment. It does not show that HCPS beat charter schools either. District-run enrollment fell every year we could check from 2018 to 2026. The charter share rose from about 11.5% to about 16.4%. Voucher demand nearly doubled after HB1 in 2023. HCPS also closed schools in 2023, 2024 and 2026.

The exception is narrow. In August 2026 HCPS *projected* a small increase, under the TBT headline "Hillsborough schools anticipate small enrollment increase this year." The projection was wrong: enrollment came in down about 2,650 students. The user may be remembering that headline. Two other partial gains are real but small: new K-8 schools (+2,292 students, 2022-23 to 2023-24) and a 2026 magnet/CTE expansion.

No Tampa Bay district (Hillsborough, Pinellas, Pasco) has shown an enrollment turnaround. We found no report of any of them signing a pay-per-recruit contract. Those contracts are in Central and North Florida: Orange, Osceola and Leon.

### Vouchers (HB1 universal ESA, signed March 2023)
- Hillsborough voucher requests rose from 10,927 to 18,784 (+72%) between the prior year and 2023-24. In Pinellas they rose from 6,242 to 11,434 (WUSF, Aug 10 2023).
- In 2023-24, 867 former HCPS students newly used FTC/FES scholarships at private schools, which is 0.39% of FTE (Step Up for Students data via TBT). Most new voucher users statewide were already in private school or homeschool.
- Hillsborough is reported to have more homeschooled students than any other US county (Fox13, Feb 2024). Homeschool growth was cited again for 2025.
- In 2026 Superintendent Van Ayres said a "competitive choice environment" makes it hard to predict who will leave through vouchers.

## 3. Closures and repurposing

- 2023: Just Elementary closed (4-3 vote in May 2023). It is being merged into a rebuilt Stewart Magnet Middle.
- June 2023 final plan: 6 closures affecting 15,277 students, saving about $13.5M a year. A larger 7-school option, saving about $31M, was rejected. Adams, Monroe and McLane middle schools and Cleveland and Kimbell elementary schools closed in summer 2024. Adams is planned to reopen as a college-prep academy.
- Empty seats in 2022: about 22.0k elementary, 10.1k middle and 2.3k high. 83 schools were at or below 70% capacity and 16 were at or below 50%. The board refused to sell surplus campuses.
- March-April 2026: three closures were approved for 2026-27. Graham Elementary had 153 students enrolled out of 647 zoned and was below 40% capacity. Madison Middle was below 60% capacity, which reversed part of the 2024 Monroe move. Pizzo K-8 (about 900 students) closed because USF's lease costs rose nearly tenfold, about $8M over the term. Young Magnet Middle is at 29% capacity.
- After the closures, enrollment kept falling (2024 → 2025: -7,177 district-run; 2026: down again). Closures did not reverse the decline, and they were not meant to. Their purpose was cost savings.

## 4. Other Tampa Bay districts (possible conflation)

- **Pinellas:** 77,854 → 74,203 (-3,651) in 2025-26, with kindergarten -9.3%. Down about 3,800 more in 2026. In September 2026 it proposed closing 7 schools, saving about $25M a year and removing about 5,000 empty seats; the board votes Oct 13 2026. Births fell from 9,541 in 2006 to 7,010 in 2024. No turnaround.
- **Pasco:** The fastest-growing county, yet district enrollment fell 1,393 in 2025-26 while vouchers grew 1,965 and charters 941. Down 787 more in fall 2025 and 2,518 in 2026. Nearly 500 jobs were cut in June 2026. Closures: Hudson (2020), Locke (2023), Calusa (2025), and Gulfside planned. No turnaround.
- **Polk:** Nearly flat (114,946 → 114,627). Charter enrollment rose while district enrollment fell.

- **$935** is confirmed as the Florida rate (Orange, Osceola). **$985 was not found** in any source. The closest out-of-state figure is $953 (Washington Twp). Treat $985 as unverified.
- Cost per *net* student gained cannot be calculated, because every contracting district still lost students overall. The per-student fee of about $935 is roughly 10% of about $7,000-9,000 in FEFP revenue. If the recruits are real and stay a full year, the gross return looks positive. But "recruited" means 30 days of attendance; counterfactual enrollment (families who would have returned anyway) is not measured; and retention past the payment trigger is unreported. **Vendor caution:** recruit counts come from Caissa or from district staff reporting Caissa's numbers. No independent evaluation exists.

- Charter share figures for 2024-25: **MED** (derived).

## 7. Lessons for Minneapolis
1. **No Florida model has shown a net turnaround.** Under universal vouchers plus falling births, every Tampa Bay district shrank. The best realistic outcome is to slow the decline. Frame goals as retention and slowing losses, not growth.
2. **Recruitment buys gross recoveries, not net gains.** Orange recovered 1,932 students and still lost about 5,500. If MPS hires a recruiter, require: (a) a full-year or Oct 1 count payment trigger, not 30 days; (b) exclusion of students who would have enrolled anyway, such as prior-year re-enrollers and siblings; (c) a cap; (d) independent reporting of net change and retention.
3. **Price benchmark:** about $935 per student in Florida ($675-953 elsewhere), against roughly $7-9k in per-pupil revenue. In Minnesota per-pupil revenue is higher, so the gross math looks better, which makes the counterfactual question even more important.
4. **Distinctive programs move students within the district more than they add students to it.** HCPS K-8s grew while traditional elementary and middle schools shrank by more. New magnets need busing to reach families: HCPS's new Montessori has none.
5. **Right-size alongside any recruitment.** HCPS closed schools in waves (2023, 2024, 2026) after earlier boards rejected bigger plans and refused to sell surplus campuses. That led to repeated rounds of disruption and even reversals: Madison/Monroe was reversed within 2 years.
6. **Projections fail in a choice market.** HCPS predicted a 2026 gain and lost students. Budget against conservative scenarios.
7. **Don't cite Tampa as a success story.** If the board or the public hears "Tampa turned it around," the record says otherwise.
